Black Holes and the Cosmos: An Introduction

Black holes are among the most fascinating and extreme objects in the universe. They form when massive stars exhaust their nuclear fuel and collapse under their own gravity. When a star with a mass more than about twenty times that of our Sun reaches the end of its life, the core collapses catastrophically, triggering a supernova explosion. What remains is an object so dense that not even light can escape its gravitational pull. This boundary, beyond which nothing can return, is called the event horizon.

The concept of a black hole was first predicted by Einstein's general theory of relativity, which describes how mass warps the fabric of spacetime. At the center of a black hole lies the singularity, a point of infinite density where the laws of physics as we know them break down. Physicists are still working to reconcile general relativity with quantum mechanics to understand what actually happens at this extreme boundary.

Neutron stars are closely related to black holes. When a star of moderate mass collapses, it may not have enough mass to form a black hole. Instead, the core compresses into a neutron star — an object roughly the size of a city but containing more mass than the Sun. Neutron stars spin rapidly and emit beams of radiation, sometimes observed as pulsars. The density of a neutron star is so extreme that a teaspoon of its material would weigh billions of tonnes on Earth.

Galaxy formation is one of the grand questions of modern astrophysics. Galaxies began forming hundreds of millions of years after the Big Bang, as gravity drew together clouds of hydrogen and helium gas. Over billions of years, these clouds collapsed into the first stars, which seeded the universe with heavier elements through nuclear fusion and supernova explosions. Dark matter played a crucial role in galaxy formation, providing the gravitational scaffolding around which ordinary matter could accumulate.

The cosmic expansion of the universe was discovered by Edwin Hubble in the 1920s. He observed that distant galaxies are moving away from us, and the farther they are, the faster they recede. This expansion is described by Hubble's Law. In 1998, astronomers discovered that the expansion of the universe is actually accelerating, driven by a mysterious force called dark energy, which makes up approximately 68 percent of the total energy content of the universe.

The observable universe extends about 46 billion light-years in every direction. It contains roughly two trillion galaxies, each containing hundreds of billions of stars. The scale is almost incomprehensible. Our own Milky Way galaxy is a barred spiral galaxy about 100,000 light-years across, containing between 100 and 400 billion stars. Our Sun is an ordinary star located about 26,000 light-years from the galactic center.

Gravitational waves, first directly detected in 2015 by the LIGO experiment, opened a new window on the universe. These ripples in spacetime are produced by violent cosmic events such as the merger of two black holes or two neutron stars. The detection confirmed another prediction of general relativity and earned the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2017.
